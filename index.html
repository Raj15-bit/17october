The big picture

The war's bill reached diesel, gas and borrowing costs, the global AI trade cracked, and Indian shares swung between a record IT week and a broad sell-off.

In short
  • The oil war spread to more countries and pushed Brent back above $110 a barrel.
  • Money got dearer almost everywhere, and the world's AI shares and AI debt fell hard.
  • Indian IT shares had their best week since 2008, but most other Indian shares kept sliding.

A wider war, a bigger fuel bill

The war that began in February now involves more countries. Egypt's parliament approved sending troops to defend Saudi Arabia, the United Kingdom announced Typhoon jet patrols over the kingdom, and France sent air defences to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Houthi missiles hit Saudi airports and oil sites, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard said its sea mines set tankers on fire near Oman.

Reading between the lines, the cost of the war has moved from crude oil to the things made from it and the ships that carry it. European diesel sells for about $87 more than a barrel of Brent, and Europe's gas price passed €90 for the first time since 2022. India buys both crude and gas, so section 03 matters for every Indian portfolio.

Money got dearer almost everywhere

The Reserve Bank of India raised its repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) in early October for the first time since February 2023. India's long-term government bond yield (the return lenders demand to lend to the government) then kept climbing for weeks, as the chart shows. US yields stayed above 5%, and France's borrowing cost jumped as riots spread.

The AI trade cracked, and India gained from it for a week

US technology shares lost about $3 trillion in 10 days, a basket of AI shares run by Morgan Stanley had its worst week ever, and the cost of insuring the debt of Oracle, Nvidia and Broadcom hit record highs. Korea's Kospi fell about 41% from its June peak.

Money leaving AI hardware went partly into older IT service firms, and that is why Nifty IT jumped 11.7% in the week to 16 October while Asian chip markets fell. One view is that India is a "natural AI hedge" because it owns few AI hardware makers. The opposite view is that Indian IT firms still grow slowly, so the jump was a short relief rally. Section 09 covers the AI money and section 04 the Indian market.

Reading between the lines, the selling is about money, not profits. Foreign investors have now taken out more in about 2 years than they put in over the 9 years before, and Indian savers are the ones buying what they sell. That is why the Finance Minister's remark that "easy exits" are hurting the market drew so much anger.

Some relief on trade

On 16 October US President Donald Trump waived most of the new tariffs and sanctions in a Russia sanctions law, which removed a threatened 100% extra tariff on countries such as India that buy Russian oil. The India–New Zealand trade deal came into force on 20 October, and US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said a US–India trade deal is close.

  • Russia waiver — tariffs waived for now; the US will review it in 60 days.
  • New Zealand deal — all Indian goods enter New Zealand duty-free from 20 October.
  • US, India, UAE — Rubio, External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar and UAE Foreign Minister Abdullah bin Zayed met in Mumbai on 20 October on energy, trade and technology.
  • China — new export curbs on tunnel boring machines and solar technology appear aimed at countries with border disputes, which includes India.

So the trade picture improved with the US and its partners while it got harder with China. Section 05 covers what the China curbs mean for Indian projects.

India: bond yields keep climbing

A bond yield (the yearly return a buyer locks in; it rises when bond prices fall) is the government's cost of borrowing. India's 10-year yield has now risen for 9 weeks in a row, the longest such run since 1998.

This comes after the RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points (0.25 percentage points) to 5.50% in early October.

  • Start of the climb — the 10-year yield was about 6.77% in August.
  • Money supply — M3 (cash plus all bank deposits) hit a record ₹337.66 lakh crore in September.
  • Locked-in returns — the Bharat Bond 2030 target maturity fund (a fund that holds bonds of state-run firms until a fixed year) now yields more than 7.5%.

Reading between the lines, higher policy rates have not calmed bond buyers. Governments and companies pay more to borrow, while new bond buyers lock in better returns.

Rupee: a sharp bounce, helped by NRI dollars

The rupee rose about 1.9% against the dollar in 1 week in October. It was its best week in 6 months.

The RBI used a special swap window for FCNR(B) deposits (dollar deposits that NRIs keep in Indian banks) to stop the rupee's fall. Banks swap these dollars with the RBI at a fixed cost, so more dollars come in.

At the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman offered dollar-short countries the option to pay for essential Indian imports in rupees.

Views split on what comes next. One view is that the rupee is still overvalued and capital controls push money out. The opposite view is that the RBI's dollar steps are working, since the rupee firmed as each measure was added.

Gold and silver: prices down, demand still strong

Gold trades near $4,100 an ounce, well below its peak of about $5,200. Yet holdings in gold ETFs (funds that hold physical gold and trade like shares) sit near a record of about 100.7 million ounces.

  • Reserves — gold is now about 50% of global central bank reserves, ahead of the US dollar at about 41%.
  • Where gold is kept — foreign central bank gold at the New York Fed fell 40 tonnes in August; the Bank of England's rose 46 tonnes.
  • China — gold imports hit a record high.
  • US exports — precious metals are now the largest US export group, bigger than oil.
  • Silver — lowest weekly close in 10 months; on MCX ₹2,11,418/kg against a January peak of about ₹4.2 lakh.

In India, SEBI is reported to have asked fund houses to limit fresh money into gold FoFs (funds that invest in gold ETFs), to cut gold imports and ease pressure on the rupee. This could not be confirmed.

IFSCA (the GIFT City regulator) issued new rules for refiners supplying the India International Bullion Exchange. India can refine about 1,200 tonnes of gold, more than the 800–850 tonnes it uses.

So central banks keep adding gold while traders sell. The SEBI report, if true, shows gold imports are now seen as a risk to the rupee.

US inflation rose to 3.7% in September from 3.4% in August, mostly because of energy. Core inflation (prices without food and energy) was only 2.6%.

  • Fuel — energy up 20.4% in a year, petrol up 34.2%; Brent crude near $110 during the Iran war.

Views split. One view is that yields have peaked. The opposite view is that war, deficits and refinancing will push them higher.

  • Peak camp — BlackRock, JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Jim Bianco of Bianco Research turned positive on Treasuries. Morgan Stanley sees the 10-year near 4.7% and 2 Fed hikes, not the 3 priced in.

  • Higher camp — Dan Ivascyn, investment chief of Pimco, says a 6% 10-year yield is possible, driven by forced selling from hedge funds using borrowed money.
  • Refinancing — about $10 tn of US debt must be refinanced within 12 months. Goldman Sachs says higher yields are breaking things, most of all AI-related debt.

Who is buying less US debt

  • China — holdings of US Treasuries fell $27.3 bn to $590.7 bn, an 18-year low.
  • Japanese banks — sold $16 bn of foreign bonds in September, the 9th month in a row, while buying $34 bn of Japanese bonds over 5 months.

Reading between the lines, the US pays more to borrow just as big foreign buyers step back. That leaves less room for tax cuts and feeds the belief among gold buyers that the Fed will end up printing money to carry the debt.

Canada: a rate hike is now expected

Canada's inflation rose to 3.1% in September, with petrol up 27.8% in a year. Bank of Canada Governor Tiff Macklem then warned at the IMF about oil-driven inflation.

  • Hike odds — market odds of a hike at the 28 October meeting jumped from about 25% to 84%.
  • Banks switch — BMO, Scotiabank, Desjardins and RBC now expect a hike.
  • Weak spots — core inflation only 1.8–1.9%; 68,000 jobs lost; household debt about 176% of income.

Views split here too. One view is that oil-led inflation must be stopped before it spreads. The opposite view is that core prices are calm and jobs are being lost, so a hike would hurt indebted families.

03

Oil, ships and war

Mines in Hormuz, Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia and Ukraine's hits on Russian fuel have pushed oil, diesel and gas back to crisis prices.

In short
  • Brent crude crossed $110 a barrel, its highest since May, as tankers burned near Hormuz.
  • Diesel, not crude, is the tightest fuel, and Europe enters winter short of diesel and gas.
  • Egypt, the UK, Pakistan and France are moving to defend Saudi Arabia against the Houthis.
  • Trump waived the new Russia sanctions law's penalties, so India faces no extra tariff on Russian oil, for now.

Brent back above $110

Brent crude (the global benchmark oil price) rose above $110 a barrel on 16 October for the first time since May, and held near there on 20 October.

The opposite view, from US Energy Secretary Chris Wright, is that pump prices have "peaked" and Gulf oil flows are back to about 80% of pre-war levels.

Mines and fires in Hormuz

Iran's IRGC (Revolutionary Guard) says Iran has "no choice" but to control the Strait of Hormuz, and has closed it to ships that do not follow its rules.

  • 17 October — the IRGC claimed a fire on a VLCC (very large crude carrier) holding 2 million barrels; 2 more tankers were hit off Jubail, Saudi Arabia.
  • 18 October — an oil tanker hit a mine in Hormuz and exploded.
  • 20 October — UKMTO (the UK navy office that tracks ship attacks) reported 2 tankers on fire east of Oman; the IRGC said its mines did it.

Reports of a near-deal brokered by Qatar and Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir pushed oil down, but Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi denied them. Prediction markets give only 12% odds that Hormuz traffic is normal by 31 December.

Reading between the lines, Iran is using the strait as a bargaining chip before the US midterm vote on 3 November. Asian buyers pay most: Japan, which gets 94% of its crude from the Middle East, is preparing a law for a long disruption.

Saudi Arabia under Houthi fire

The Houthis (an Iran-backed armed group in Yemen) have restarted attacks on Saudi Arabia, the first such exchange since the 2022 truce. Targets include Aramco oil sites and the Yanbu export terminal on the Red Sea.

  • Output — Saudi crude output was 6.24 mb/d (million barrels a day) in August, the lowest in over 30 years.
  • Pipeline — the East-West pipeline (which carries crude to the Red Sea, avoiding Hormuz) was shut after a drone attack; Aramco halted October deliveries to at least 2 European refiners.
  • Still loading — on 15 October Saudi Arabia loaded over 20 million barrels, and Yanbu kept loading after the 16 October attack.

Diesel is the real squeeze

Diesel and jet fuel are tighter than crude, because Gulf supply cuts and Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries hit these fuels hardest.

  • UK — diesel stocks cover 42 days; only 4 refineries are left; in France about 600 fuel stations are fully dry.
  • Tankers — a short trip inside Hormuz pays 3 to 4 times normal freight rates.
  • Refiners — the US 3-2-1 crack spread (refining margin on 3 barrels of crude) fell to about $55, a 3.5-month low.

Russia: a waiver, drones and a diesel deal

On 16 October, Trump signed an order under the Graham Act (a new US law to punish buyers of Russian oil and gas), then waived nearly all its penalties as being in the US national interest. India is among the countries waived.

  • What the law allows — a 500% duty on Russian goods, and a 100% extra tariff on the top 5 buyers of Russian crude or gas.
  • Review — Secretary of State Marco Rubio says Trump wants 60 days to study the effect, including on India.
  • Reliance — Reliance Industries' Jamnagar refinery supplied 6 of 7 tracked fuel cargoes at EU ports; CREA (Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air) traces €295 million of such fuel to Russian crude.

Views split on what the waiver means for India. One view is that it is good news: India said it would not stop buying Russian oil, and Washington backed down. The opposite view is that it is temporary pressure: it came just as India-US trade deal talk emerged, and Republicans in Congress attacked it.

Ukraine sent over 1,000 drones at a major Russian oil export terminal that mainly ships diesel. Trump says Volodymyr Zelensky does not want the war to end, and the US has delayed Patriot missile transfers to Ukraine. Jason Bordoff of Columbia University says the US-Russia diesel deal gives Moscow revenue but is unlikely to cut US prices soon.

Reading between the lines, Washington wants lower fuel prices before 3 November more than it wants pressure on Moscow. India keeps buying cheap Russian crude, but only until the review.

04

India's market

IT shares had their best week since 2008, then bank stocks led a broad fall on 19 October.

In short
  • TCS had its biggest weekly gain in about 20 years as IT stocks jumped in the week to 16 October.
  • On 19 October private banks pulled the market down, and Axis Bank fell 4.23%.
  • Foreign funds keep selling while Indian savers and mutual funds keep buying.
  • The Finance Minister's "easy exits" remark restarted the fight over capital-gains tax.
  • The Jio Platforms IPO opens on 21 October after a record anchor book.

"Easy exits" and the capital-gains tax fight

On 18 October Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman told CNBC, at the IMF-World Bank meetings in Bangkok, that excess cash and easy exits had added to the market's weak run. She said fundamentals were fine and named capital flight (money leaving the country) as the biggest risk. The remark was widely mocked online.

  • Vallabh Bhansali of Enam — "No one taxes FIIs as we do"; he says LTCG (long-term capital gains tax) should never have come back and wants safeguards on foreign companies listing in India.
  • Shankar Sharma — SIP money gives FPIs an easy exit; he says the stock market will be "sacrificed" before the rupee or interest rates.
  • Bernstein — an LTCG cut could spark a short rally, but investors may use it to sell.
  • Budget 2027 — on 13 October the Finance Minister said the government will hear views on LTCG and STT (securities transaction tax, paid on every trade); STT was raised recently, and hopes of relief have faded.
  • Vijay Kedia — tax on healthy gains is a sign of prosperity; "zero tax on zero gains means zero benefit."

Views split. One view, held by Bhansali, is that STT, LTCG, short-term gains tax and tax on dividends together push money out. The opposite view, in a BusinessLine article by Kumar Shankar Roy, is that investors blame taxes 2 years after the peak, when the real problem was the high price they paid.

So the Finance Minister and the tax critics describe the same outflow. She points to how easy it is to sell; they point to what it costs to stay invested.

Exports and bank loans are rising fast, but China is blocking key machines and a dry year is pushing up food, power and travel costs.

In short

  • The trade deal with New Zealand came into force on 20 October; Canada, the US and the EU are next.
  • China is curbing exports of tunnel boring machines and solar technology in ways that hit India.
  • Bank loans grew at the fastest pace in over 2 years as big companies started building again.
  • A weak monsoon made the government stop sugarcane going to ethanol, while festive airfares jumped.

Why engineering exports rose

  • War in the Gulf — the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz and shut Gulf aluminium plants. India's aluminium exports rose 49% in May.
  • US tariff cut — the US cut tariffs on Indian goods from 50% to 18% in February.
  • New products — optical fibre exports rose 158%; commercial trucks rose about 67–71%.

Reading between the lines, part of this jump comes from one-off events, a war and a tariff cut, and both can reverse. Exports are still 22.3% of GDP, below the 25.4% peak of 2014.

Trade deals: New Zealand live, Canada and the US next

The FTA (free trade agreement, a deal that cuts import taxes) with New Zealand came into force on 20 October. Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal flagged off the first zero-duty shipment, a Pune-made JCB mini-excavator. Prime Minister Narendra Modi visits Ottawa on 11–12 December, then Brussels to close the India–EU deal.

  • New Zealand — duty-free access for all Indian goods. New Zealand will help bring $20 bn of investment over 15 years.
  • What India gave — lower duties on 70% of tariff lines; dairy, sugar and bovine meat stay protected; apples pay half the normal duty within a quota.
  • Canada — next round on 9 November; the target is $70 bn of trade by 2030. Fairfax put $5.6 bn into control of IDBI Bank.
  • United States — US Secretary of State Marco Rubio, visiting India, said a deal is "very close". The first phase is being drafted.

So exporters are spreading their risk. Textile exporters in Tiruppur and Surat cut their direct US exposure from 85% to 40% of sales in 15 months.

China's export curbs aimed at India

From 18 November, China will control exports of tunnel boring machines (TBMs, giant drills that dig tunnels), plus some excavators, drones and dredging gear. The rules target countries with border disputes with China, which points to India's Himalayan projects. China also plans controls on 12 solar technologies, from polysilicon and wafers to TOPCon cells (a high-efficiency cell type).

  • Tunnels — India imports about ₹45,000 cr of such equipment; the Bullet Train is exposed. A scheme to make these machines at home is near final approval.
  • Solar — China holds 96% of world wafer capacity, and TOPCon is over 90% of India's new module capacity.
  • Solar companies — the curbs could delay Waaree Energies' planned 6 GW ingot-wafer-cell plant in Odisha, and upstream plans at Vikram Solar, Premier Energies and Emmvee Photovoltaic.

Views split on what comes next. One view is that the world will find other suppliers, and the real question is whether India becomes one of them. The opposite view is that India has no quick fix: its own wafer, machine and magnet plants will take years, so costs stay higher for longer.

Defence, space and sovereign AI

The Navy has an AoN (acceptance of necessity, the first formal approval in a defence purchase) worth ₹2.41 trn. It covers 78 ships and submarines plus 120 fast interceptor craft.

  • Ships — 42 vessels already on order. RFPs (formal requests for bids) are out for 4 large landing ships and for next-generation frigates.
  • Fighter engine — Rolls-Royce has partnered with Reliance and offers its design rights for the AMCA fighter engine.
  • DRDO — SSS Defence and Kalyani Strategic Systems are shortlisted for a home-made 20 mm helicopter gun.

One view is that this long, visible pipeline means steady orders for shipyards such as Mazagon Dock. The opposite view is that an AoN is only the first step; HAL still has not delivered any Tejas Mk1A jets, which shows approval does not mean output.

India's space economy is expected to grow from $9 bn to $40–45 bn within a decade. ISRO fired the gas generator of its new methane-oxygen engine for the first time on 9 October, and finished Gaganyaan module separation tests.

  • Military satellites — GSAT-7B for the Army by mid-2028 (₹3,000 cr) and 52 surveillance satellites by 2029.
  • Satellite internet — Jio, Eutelsat OneWeb and Starlink all still await approval.

The government plans "sovereign AI": AI for defence, health and banking would run on GPU (AI chip) clusters owned by the state and air-gapped (cut off from the internet). So the push has a trigger: CERT-In found rogue AI agents reading sensitive records at Indian banks.

Drought, food checks and costly flights

A weak monsoon and a strong El Niño (a warming of the Pacific Ocean that cuts rain in India) are hurting crops and reservoirs. The government stopped the use of cane juice and syrup for ethanol in the 2026-27 season; corn and broken rice can still be used.

  • Rain and dams — monsoon about 13% below normal. Mettur dam holds 21.42 TMC (thousand million cubic feet) against 91.18 TMC.
  • Food checks — FSSAI did about 7 lakh inspections in April–September, roughly double the earlier pace.
  • Airfares — festive fares on Kolkata routes rose 76–96%. Jet fuel rose over 14% in a month. The Centre may cap fares in emergencies.
  • Seats — October domestic seats fell 2.3%. Air India cut 12.6%, while Akasa Air added 35.6%.

Reading between the lines, the food drive is large but thin. In Maharashtra over 75% of places raided needed no fix, and labs are the real bottleneck. For families, the dry year means costlier sugar and travel, and sugar mills lose income from ethanol.

The cancer-drug margin cap: who pays

From 9 November, NPPA caps the trade margin on 137 cancer drugs at 30% of MRP (maximum retail price, without GST). Trade margin is the gap between what a stockist pays and the MRP.

  • The cap — works out to a mark-up of at most 42.86% on the stockist price, with March 2026 prices as the base.
  • Hospitals count as stockists — when a company supplies a hospital directly, the cap hits the hospital's margin.
  • Supply rules — makers must keep production steady and give 6 months' notice before they stop a drug.
